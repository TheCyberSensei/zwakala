## Clarification and revised interpretation: community reporting and response

**9 November 2026 — this addendum qualifies the earlier recommendation below.** The owner clarified that Direction B principally concerns ordinary South Africans sharing scams, attempted fraud and digital-safety experiences so others can recognise threats, contribute relevant information, reveal recurring concerns and bring them to institutions able to investigate or respond. HelloPeter and community reporting of a leaking pipe illustrate the intended reporting-to-action value. This is the owner's original concept clarified, not a replacement idea generated by Codex.

The earlier assessment placed too much emphasis on an authoring/editorial-control need. Authorship, consent and privacy remain necessary safeguards, but they need not be the primary user benefit. The original reasoning and feasibility outline below are preserved as the earlier assessment; they should not override this clarification or be read as an approved build specification. The earlier handover also predates this clarification.

The [focused research extension](community-reporting-intelligence.md) finds evidence of reporting barriers, established public/private reporting models and operational use of consumer intelligence. It also finds important limits: existing platforms already offer aggregation and AI summaries; crowd verification can delay warnings; AI can misclassify allegations; and institutions need a usable receiving process. Public disclosure is a choice to examine, not a prerequisite for collective intelligence. Lack of a directly matching controlled study is not grounds to dismiss the direction, but neither proves demand nor effectiveness.

**Revised conditional recommendation:** evaluate the owner's reporting, community-intelligence and accountability objectives on their own terms. Test whether accessible narratives, discovery of genuinely related experiences, qualified warnings and visible follow-up form a useful journey for a specific initial group. Discovery & Audience and Voices & Languages may be central alongside Truth, Authorship & Ownership. The brief can be served by helping lived experiences become understandable, discoverable and actionable; a generic reporting form or opaque risk score would provide weaker evidence of that fit.

The earlier findings on mixed narrative-learning effects, uncertain urgency causality and BankBox's limits still stand. They do not establish whether the clarified reporting mechanism is valuable. A small, approved demonstration with fictional cases can test routing, visibility choices, corroboration and a simulated response without promising real institutional cooperation or fraud prevention. No large-scale study is demanded before prototyping.

The next discussion should identify **whose reporting problem and which part of the experience-to-response journey to demonstrate**, with what public/private choices and evidence limits. The owner retains final direction and scope selection; no capstone has been selected and no prototype implementation is authorised.

## Earlier recommendation and its conditions

**Prioritise a discussion of Direction B as a tool for people to author, review and control their own digital-safety experiences. Keep Direction A as a valuable, separate problem-validation track.** This is conditional on confirming an actual author-side need and willing contributors. It is not a conclusion that storytelling prevents more fraud than instruction, or that contributors want to make painful experiences public.

This preference follows from three reasons:

1. The owner's B already concerns lived experience, language and ownership. Helping its authors fits the actual storytelling brief more directly than a banking tutor.
2. Existing local lessons and scam scenarios mean that another lesson chatbot or story feed needs a much stronger unmet-need argument. Author control is a distinct question worth validating, not an asserted market gap.
3. Narrative effectiveness is mixed. A useful proof of concept can demonstrate faithful authorship and informed control without pretending to establish reduced victimisation. If author demand is absent, do not manufacture it merely to fit the competition.

Direction A addresses documented concerns, but its exact audience, failed tasks and causal mechanisms remain uncertain. A task-specific simulator might be worth developing independently after validation; it should not be relabelled a storytelling tool by adding a decorative narrative.

Likewise, high story engagement is not enough: the matched-content video experiment found no clear narrative advantage in intentions. ## AI-assisted refinements of Direction A

**Refinement:** replace a broad “digital literacy for South Africans” target with one observed task and a defined support context. Investigate where a person loses safe control—understanding a request, authenticating, reviewing a recipient, or choosing help—before deciding which task merits implementation. These examples derive from the owner's stated concerns; none is selected here.

Recruit by actual needs and device access rather than age alone. Include people who successfully use help, non-users and younger adults where relevant. Compare a plain official guide, facilitated practice and unassisted practice. Measure errors, safe-help choices and delayed transfer, not just increased confidence. BankBox suggests a practice approach but has important desktop and participant-selection differences. [BankBox protocol](https://celinelatulipe.net/the-bankbox-study-practicing-online-banking-in-a-simulated-environment/).

A credible journey would introduce a clearly labelled simulation, let the learner practise a bounded task with fictitious values, explain errors and offer a second task to check transfer. No bank login, real OTP, account connection or transaction execution is necessary. A deterministic state machine can do this; an AI tutor is optional and needs a demonstrated advantage over reviewed explanations. An assistant that advises live transactions or handles credentials would introduce disproportionate risk and is outside this proof of concept.

**Decision:** preserve this refinement for independent research. It does not by itself satisfy the storytelling brief. Do not add a story wrapper merely to make it eligible.

## AI-assisted refinements of Direction B

**Refinement:** focus the original community-experience direction on the contributor's ability to tell an account in their own terms, decide what may be shared, review any transformation and preserve attribution or chosen pseudonymity. Audience learning remains an important secondary hypothesis.

Start with a small, willing group whose story-authoring and sharing difficulties can be discussed directly. Potential contributors need not all be older or fraud victims; the owner's broader concern includes everyday online vulnerability. Avoid requiring disclosure of financial losses as a condition of participation. Determine preferred language and audience rather than assuming a single “community” or an all-language launch.

The genuine connection between A and B is the human experience of seeking help, uncertainty and decisions around digital finance. A person may choose to tell such an experience. That does not justify converting banking survey answers into public stories, importing the unfinished interview, or inventing experiences to populate a product.

### Fit to each storytelling track

| Track | Genuine fit within the owner's B | Evidence or condition required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voices & Languages | Help an author express an account and review an optional translation without losing meaning or voice. | Bilingual checking for the selected language pair, including mixed-language meaning; no claim of universal coverage. |
| Discovery & Audience | Help an author make an approved version understandable and available to their intended audience. | Confirm where that audience actually encounters stories; recommendations or a social network are not necessary for the initial proof. |
| Creation & Craft | Support drafting, revision and preparation of a shareable account while keeping the person as author. | Demonstrate that the workflow helps the author, rather than producing a replacement story. |
| Truth, Authorship & Ownership | Preserve provenance, approved edits, attribution choices and specific reuse permissions. | Distinguish a personal account from independently verified facts; approval is not proof that every event occurred. |

Creation & Craft and Truth, Authorship & Ownership offer the clearest initial fit. Voices & Languages is credible only at a scope that can be reviewed. Discovery should follow a real audience need rather than becoming an algorithmic feed by default. These are assessment recommendations, not selected submission tracks.

## A bounded proof of concept, if the owner selects B

The following is a feasibility outline, not a build specification or authorisation.

**Core journey:** a contributor writes a private draft → reviews sensitive details and intended audience → optionally requests an assistive transformation → compares changes against the original → accepts/rejects edits → approves a specific version for an explicit use → previews or exports that version with its provenance and permissions. A reviewer can flag a safety claim without silently rewriting the account.

| Necessary to demonstrate | Optional after need is established | Outside the initial proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original and revised text kept distinct; explicit approval before output; clear personal-account labels; consent/use choices; accessible preview and export; a correction/withdrawal explanation | Recorded narration, human-reviewed translation, searchable approved material, a bounded AI editing assistant, safe decision prompts for readers | Open public uploads, uncontrolled social sharing, live banking help, money recovery, automatic fact certification, automated publication, recommendation algorithms, training models on participant stories |

A simple responsive web interface with structured story/version/permission records can demonstrate the workflow. A local or controlled demo can avoid public accounts and production hosting. Real multi-user private drafts would require server-side authorisation, secure storage and deletion/retention controls; browser-only storage on shared devices should not be presented as secure private storage. No framework, hosting provider or model dependency is selected by this review.

Use owner-authored, clearly labelled demonstration material until genuine accounts are separately supplied with suitable permission. A fictional demonstration cannot establish contributor demand or real-world effectiveness. Do not claim that deleting a local version recalls copies already downloaded or shared elsewhere.

### Accessibility, language and mobile requirements

For either direction, prefer short steps, plain wording, readable contrast, meaningful labels, visible focus, keyboard/screen-reader access, zoom/reflow and adequately spaced touch targets. Avoid timed interaction unless it is a deliberately approved research condition. Provide text alternatives for audio/video and no autoplay. These are proposed acceptance criteria informed by [W3C WCAG 2.2, Recommendation 12 December 2024](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/), not a claim of conformance before testing.

Start with the language(s) supported by available human reviewers. Preserve original mixed-language text and allow an author to reject a translation. Speech input may reduce typing effort but can create accent, code-switching, connectivity and privacy errors; it is not an automatic accessibility solution. Test on relevant low-cost phones and constrained connections. Download size and recovery from interruptions are part of usability, not optional polish.

### AI: justified only for a measured assistance benefit

Potential uses within B are suggested organisation, plain-language alternatives or a draft translation. Keep the original, show proposed changes and require author approval. The model must not invent events, strengthen uncertain allegations, generate testimony or pronounce a story true. Human review is necessary for meaning, cultural nuance and claims; automated redaction is an aid, not a guarantee.

Compare the same task without runtime AI. Keep AI only if it demonstrably reduces effort or improves accessibility while preserving factual fidelity and author control. In A, reviewed explanations and a deterministic simulator may be sufficient. Connectivity, service pricing, authorised delegation and community trust are not problems a language model resolves alone.

If AI is later selected, use server-side credentials, minimal approved input, explicit handling of external processing, and bounded output schemas. Treat story content as untrusted input: it must not instruct the model to bypass consent or expose other drafts. Redaction before external processing and permission checks need failure tests. No provider, language accuracy, retention policy or pricing is assumed here; verify them before selection.

### Privacy, security and ownership

Separate permission to participate from permission to publish, translate or send material to an external AI service. Minimise identifiable incident details and third-party accusations; protect drafts and consent records outside Git. Establish who may review a story and how an author can correct it or stop future use. A pseudonym does not guarantee anonymity. Public distribution and cross-border processing need a separate review against the actual intended data flows. [POPIA, sections 10–14, 19–22 and 72](https://www.justice.gov.za/legislation/acts/2013-004.pdf).

Human control must be more than a checkbox. Rural South African storytelling research documents tensions between protection and an author's wishes. A reviewer should explain risks and discuss alternatives rather than assuming either forced publicity or imposed anonymity is always appropriate. [Treffry-Goatley et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8132007/).

## What a demo can establish, and what it cannot

For B, a working demonstration can show a draft remaining private, a proposed edit being rejected, an approved version retaining attribution/permission information, and an export matching the approved text. Deliberate failure cases should show that an unapproved revision cannot be released and that another person's private draft cannot be accessed. An author can assess whether the result remains their account. These are useful capabilities even without a runtime model.

For A, a demo can show safe practice and task feedback with fictitious values. Neither demo establishes national demand, long-term independence, reduced fraud or full production security.

An initial evaluation should distinguish:

1. **Author outcome:** can a person understand the sharing choices, correct a draft, reject a change and explain what remains under their control?
2. **Fidelity:** do factual details, uncertainty, sequence and meaning survive edits and any translation? Use author and bilingual review rather than an AI scoring itself.
3. **Reader outcome:** compare the same safety content as a story and as plain guidance; measure comprehension and a new safe decision, not just preference. Examine delayed recall/transfer when feasible.
4. **Harms:** overconfidence, mistaken belief that an account is verified, unwanted disclosure, distress and reluctance to correct a persuasive story.
5. **Feasibility:** time/effort, mobile readability, data use, interruptions and the availability of human review.

Agree measures before testing. A small purposive usability study can reveal problems; it cannot estimate population effect sizes. Any recruitment, interview processing or participant study needs its own approved scope. No synthetic responses should be submitted to the published research form.

## Codex's appropriate role and the next human decision

After direction approval, Codex can help turn requirements into small changes, implement and review permission boundaries, test transformations and failure cases, document dependencies and record actual verification. A demonstration can show that development evidence rather than falsely attributing the original idea or participant testimony to AI. No scaffolding or implementation has been performed for this assessment.

The decision to make now is whether **B's author-control problem is the intended capstone focus**, with A supplying a possible experience domain rather than a second application. Before building, confirm a contributor group, one concrete authoring/sharing difficulty, the initial language scope and a permitted source of demo material. If those cannot be supported, defer this refinement rather than fabricate a storytelling need; A remains worthwhile independent research.

The 11 November 2026 deadline constrains scope, not evidence standards. Reduce the demonstrated workflow or language coverage openly if needed; do not remove consent, essential security, meaningful verification or honest limitations. Final direction and scope remain the project owner's choice.
