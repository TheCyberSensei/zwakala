# Problem evidence: digital banking control and community safety stories

Research date: **9 November 2026**. This is an AI-assisted evaluation of the project owner's two original directions, not a new capstone idea or a selected project. Read alongside [existing solutions](existing-solutions.md) and the [direction assessment](capstone-direction-assessment.md).

## Findings that matter for the decision

Digital financial access, independent task completion and safe behaviour are different outcomes. South African national sources establish widespread account access alongside uneven use and infrastructure constraints. Small studies establish that assistance and credential-sharing workarounds occur; they do not establish their national prevalence or prove that assistance usually causes fraud. The owner's observations are credible research starting points, not population findings.

The hypothesis that urgent transactions cause people with low confidence to disregard security is **plausible but unproven in its full form**. Experiments on time pressure give mixed results. They do not reproduce urgent South African banking needs, informal help and financial insecurity together.

Stories can contribute to learning and reflection, but neither narrative superiority nor sustained fraud reduction is established for the proposed South African setting. More defensible initial outcomes are author control, faithful representation, comprehension and decisions in safe practice tasks. These conclusions follow from the source records below; proposed measurements are research recommendations, not findings.

## Review method and evidence boundaries

This is a focused scoping review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Searches covered South African financial inclusion and payments, informal banking help, older adults, urgency/time pressure, narrative versus factual education, failed interventions, and participatory storytelling ethics. Primary public institutions, university manuscripts, original studies and operator documentation were prioritised. Two research agents examined banking evidence and comparators; their findings were integrated with direct source inspection and the local brief.

Evidence labels mean:

- **Strong descriptive:** credible national measurement for its stated population and construct, not causal attribution.
- **Experimental:** a causal comparison within the studied task; transfer and durability remain separate questions.
- **Limited empirical:** small, selected, observational or self-reported study.
- **Operator evidence:** establishes an offering or reported activity; effectiveness requires an evaluation design.
- **Inference / hypothesis:** an interpretation or proposed mechanism still requiring testing.

Several publisher/repository requests failed or exposed only abstracts. Those limits are stated in the register. Indexed abstracts are identified as such, not presented as full-paper inspection. Publication dates are distinguished from survey years. Search coverage cannot establish that a comparable service or evaluation does not exist.

**Excluded:** live survey access, respondent data and the unfinished family interview. No participant answers, sample characteristics or research findings have been inferred from those sources.

## Direction A: what the South African evidence establishes

FinScope Consumer South Africa **2025**, released **30 July 2026**, reports 89% adult bank-account ownership and about 13 million people predominantly receiving money and immediately withdrawing it. Its nationally representative survey interviewed 5,600 adults across nine provinces. It also reports that 7% of adults experienced fraud, including illegal debit orders or phishing. These measures are not independent app-use ability or phishing-only prevalence. Cash use may be a rational response to circumstances. The same release reports service outages, fees and poor service among banking problems. [A1]

The SARB's 2023 Payments Study describes cash and debit cards as complementary and banking apps as gaining use; payment choices are associated with income and education. This challenges the assumption that non-digital use is simply a knowledge deficit. Full-report methodology could not be retrieved reliably in this review, so no cluster percentages or causal estimates are used. [A2]

Stats SA's 2024 household survey reports 96.1% of households owning a mobile phone and 82.1% having Internet access through some means, but only 17.4% fixed home Internet. Household access does not prove that an individual has an accessible smartphone, private control of it or affordable continuous data. National connectivity totals cannot establish the experience of a rural or peri-urban novice. [A3]

### Do not collapse these mechanisms into digital literacy

The following is an analytical framework for future observation, not a finding that every mechanism applies to the owner's community.

| Dimension | What to observe separately | Why the distinction changes the response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usability | Can a person locate and understand the next action, review a recipient and recover from an error? | Poor navigation or ambiguous feedback may require service redesign rather than more lessons. |
| Digital literacy | Can they use the device and explain the purpose of authentication or an OTP? | Knowledge and procedural practice may help; a knowledge quiz alone does not establish performance. |
| Financial literacy | Do they understand a payment's meaning, fees and consequences? | Knowing how to tap a button is not understanding the transaction. |
| Accessibility | Reading, vision, hearing, motor, cognitive or assistive-technology barriers, measured with the person's chosen support. | Age is a weak substitute for actual access needs; independence need not mean refusing legitimate assistance. |
| Language | Understanding of the actual task and terminology in the preferred language, including mixed-language use. | Translation and jargon simplification are different interventions; neither fixes inaccessible interaction. |
| Trust and confidence | Confidence before a task compared with observed success and willingness to check. | A confident but inaccurate person may need different support from a capable but hesitant person. |
| Security awareness | Recognition and explanation of a suspicious request. | Recognition is a possible precursor, not proof of safer decisions. |
| Security behaviour | Whether the person verifies, pauses, shares a credential or chooses a safer help channel in a safe scenario. | Measure choices and trade-offs, not just stated intentions. |
| Structural/service constraints | Data/device access, cost, service failures, merchant acceptance and available authorised help. | Education or AI cannot remove these barriers by itself. |

Small proxy studies document helpful relationships that also involve credential-sharing workarounds, including institutional and authentication barriers. Assistance is not automatically safe or exploitative. Our proposed outcome is **retaining informed, safe control**, including supported use where appropriate. [A4], [A5]

Age-only targeting is not justified. A South African continuance-intention study sampled existing users aged 45–65; it tells us little about non-users or adults over 65. Separately, an experimental phishing study found no overall accuracy difference between older and younger adults. Neither result means age-related access needs are absent. [A6], [A9]

East African research identifies adaptations involving agents, credit, identification and convenience. This supports examining workarounds as responses to service gaps, not merely ignorance. It is comparative evidence: agent-based mobile money in Kenya/Tanzania is not interchangeable with South African bank apps or ATMs. [A7]

### The urgency hypothesis: support, counterevidence and missing links

The owner's hypothesis is: when an urgently needed financial task exceeds a person's confidence or knowledge, immediate need may outweigh security concerns.

| Evidence | What it supports or challenges | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Butavicius, Taib and Han: 472-person email experiment [A8] | Imposed time pressure reduced phishing classification accuracy. Supports one possible mechanism. | Urgent financial need, unsafe assistance, low confidence, or South African banking causality. |
| Sarno and colleagues: three email-classification experiments [A9] | Time pressure did not change overall accuracy; age differences concerned caution and speed. Challenges a universal effect. | That urgency never matters in other contexts. |
| Wegier and Spaniol: two financial-choice experiments [A10] | Pressure reduced risk seeking for described losses; no pressure effect in experience-based choices. Direction depends on framing and experience. | Fraud avoidance or real-world survival decisions; participants were small undergraduate samples. |
| Proxy and agent studies [A4], [A5], [A7] | Trust, convenience, service design and support relationships can explain workarounds. | That urgency rather than these alternatives caused credential sharing. |

The full causal chain remains untested here. Future enquiry should distinguish genuine financial urgency from a scammer's manufactured deadline; confidence from demonstrated competence; and deliberate risk acceptance from misunderstanding. Compare the same task with and without time pressure, and safe-help availability with its absence. This would require a separately approved, non-financial simulation, never pressure applied to real money or credentials.

Competing explanations to investigate include a trusted helper's convenience, inaccessible authentication, inability to afford data, an outage, fear of an irreversible mistake, and a rational need for cash. Education may be useful when the barrier is learnable; it is not a substitute for affordable, usable services. Financial-education research also cautions against the opposite overstatement that education never changes behaviour: a large experimental synthesis reports average benefits, but does not validate this particular simulator or AI approach. [A11]

## Direction B: learning from experience is promising, but conditional

Three distinctions are essential: **telling one's own story versus consuming somebody else's**, **engagement versus learning**, and **learning versus behaviour outside the study**.

- A 299-response replication of security-story research found relationships between story characteristics, reported learning and retelling. It is a US survey of recalled stories, not a randomised education trial; 299 usable responses remained from 622 collected. Recall and selection limit generalisation. [B1]
- A 2023 antiphishing experiment reports better curiosity, self-efficacy and detection ability for narrative training than a non-narrative comparator. Full methods were not accessible here, so the result is supportive but cannot establish durability or local transfer. [B2]
- A 2026 randomised study of 437 US adults found both video formats improved protective intentions against a text control, but no statistically meaningful narrative-versus-didactic difference when content was aligned. Intentions were self-reported, not observed security behaviour. [B3]
- A South African maternal-health trial provides a useful local warning against equating satisfaction with learning: 204 surveyed participants, high video satisfaction, but an estimated knowledge difference of 0.28 points with a 95% uncertainty interval from −0.58 to 1.16. This is not cybersecurity evidence, but it is a relevant implementation/measurement comparison. [B4]
- An industrial study of interactive security comics found that many of 20 volunteers preferred text and found images distracting. Its specialist capture-the-flag setting does not invalidate community stories; it demonstrates that format can obstruct a task. [B5]

**Inference:** narrative safety education is most defensible when it preserves concrete decision points, actionable guidance and a credible connection to the audience, while remaining comparable against simpler instruction. Vividness alone can amplify an inaccurate causal explanation. A story about one event cannot establish how common that event is. The review does not establish which South African language, format or story source is most effective for the proposed audience.

### Community authorship is a separate, substantiated design concern

Rural South African/Kenyan storytelling research shows that local practices and social context affect tool design; an imported individual authoring workflow may fit poorly. It demonstrates situated design, not fraud prevention. [B6]

A rural South African storytelling project documents conflicts between protecting identity and respecting authors' attribution wishes. Names alone do not determine identifiability. This supports ongoing author decisions rather than blanket promises of anonymity or empowerment. [B7]

POPIA supplies relevant obligations on minimal information, specified purposes, retention, safeguards and cross-border transfers (sections 10–14, 19–22, 72). Consent is not its only lawful basis and does not remove all other obligations. For this project's proposed workflow, separate permission for research, public storytelling, translation and external AI processing is a prudent design requirement, not a claim that a checkbox guarantees legal compliance. [P1]

## Important gaps and checks before a build decision

1. **Task prevalence:** no reviewed source measures the proportion of South Africans unable to complete the owner's particular banking tasks independently. Neither account ownership nor fraud losses supplies that denominator.
2. **Local mechanisms:** rural/peri-urban, disability, literacy and language experiences need direct, consented investigation. English-language online-user samples will miss some of the people most relevant to Direction A.
3. **Age:** recruit around actual task/support needs and include younger users; do not assume all older people struggle or all younger people are safe.
4. **Behaviour:** measure delayed transfer to a new safe task and inappropriate trust as well as immediate confidence. No fraud-reduction claim follows from a successful demo or knowledge quiz.
5. **Narrative demand:** willing storytellers, the desired audience, fears about disclosure and author-control problems are not established by the current banking-staff survey.
6. **Intervention comparison:** compare assistance or practice with a clear bank guide, and narrative material with equivalent plain guidance. Separate content quality, language and delivery format.
7. **Administrative data:** SABRIC reports document substantial banking crime, but its 2024 press statement and indexed report table disagree on digital totals; the reason was not reconciled. No trend or population-victim rate is calculated here. See the [official 2024 statement](https://www.sabric.co.za/media-statement-sabric-annual-crime-statistics-2024/), [report](https://www.sabric.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/CRIME-STATISTICS-REPORT-2024.pdf) and [2025 release](https://www.sabric.co.za/sabric-release-the-2025-annual-banking-crime-statistics-report/). These are administrative/operator reporting, not evidence that education prevents losses.

The staff survey can later identify recurring observations and candidate tasks. It cannot independently establish customer motives, national prevalence or efficacy. A family interview can contribute a situated account, never a representative conclusion. Analyse both separately from this literature when explicitly provided and authorised.
