# Existing solutions and comparable work

Reviewed **9 November 2026** using public primary pages, university research and original operator reports. This comparison informs the owner's existing directions; it is not a novelty claim or an endorsement. No chatbot, bank account, scanner or research dataset was operated. Availability means an inspected public page or resource, not a completed end-to-end test.

The important distinction is between **an offering**, **people using it**, **measured learning**, and **sustained safer behaviour**. Evidence at one level does not establish the next. Where a licence or evaluation was not located, that is a review limitation, not proof of absence.

## What the main comparators establish

### BankBox: a relevant practice model, not proof that simulation solves the problem

Celine Latulipe and Mehran Hossain's Manitoba study uses fictitious balances, payments and transfers. It recruits people 65+ already comfortable online; **smartphones/tablets are unsupported**. Results remain unposted on the inspected protocol. English materials and Canadian-bank practice modes were found; AI and other languages were not established. [S1], [S2]

No demonstrated learning gain or matching source-code licence was located. Do not confuse it with an unrelated cryptocurrency project named BankBox.

**Implication:** its task-practice method is worth comparing with Direction A, but Canadian desktop interfaces and digitally comfortable participants do not validate a South African mobile novice experience. Verify reuse permission before copying software, brand assets or materials.

### Informal banking assistance: design for safe support, not an assumed ban on help

The Canadian and UK proxy studies are research comparators, not evaluated support products. No AI component is established. Their methods inform questions about consent and control; instrument reuse permissions need checking. See A4–A5 in [problem evidence](problem-evidence.md). [S3]

### Capitec MoneyUp Chat

The inspected product and developer pages describe Moola, a **menu-driven WhatsApp learning bot** with short lessons, questions and challenges covering topics such as saving, debt and fraud. The developer describes buttons/emojis and short-course interactions. “Chatbot” is not evidence of generative AI. A complete current language list was not verified. [S4], [S5]

Capitec's FY2025 sustainability report records 543,193 registered users and 275,765 completing at least one module. It describes an independent evaluation, but the inspected summary reports self-reported improvements without the evaluation methods. Treat these as operator-reported engagement and reported experience, not a causal fraud-prevention result. WhatsApp also assumes suitable access. No reusable software licence was located. [S6]

**Implication:** a generic conversational financial lesson is already represented locally. The project needs a demonstrated unmet task or author-control need rather than a chatbot as its differentiator.

### Capitec Slam the Scam

This name covers related formats rather than one fixed application: WhatsApp scenario challenges, radio programming and scam explanations. Capitec's report identifies Zulu, Xhosa and Sotho radio channels; this does not establish that all digital content is available in those languages. The 2026 page features fraud/education staff discussing scams. AI functionality is not established. [S6], [S7], [S8]

The referenced competition ended in February 2026; explanatory content remains publicly available. Reach and competition participation cannot establish safer later decisions. No controlled evaluation or open adaptation licence was located in the inspected material.

**Implication:** locally situated scam scenarios and African-language delivery already exist. Direction B should not claim novelty from those features alone. Institutional storytelling and a person's control of their own account are different user needs.

### CyberSafe Foundation: Shine Your Eye / Sabi Toolkit

This programme targets people 50+ and uses stories, experiences, videos and practical safety guidance. The operator lists **English, French, Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, Nigerian Pidgin and Swahili** resources; South African-language coverage was not verified. No AI component was established. [S9]

The public page sits under Past Programs. Its 4,078+ download counter differs from the 2022 report's 10,000+ figure; neither is a verified unique-user or outcome measure. The report also describes a seniors' community and video series. No controlled behavioural outcome study was located. Toolkit downloads are offered, but licence terms for adaptation were not established; an English PDF request failed due to retrieval size. [S9], [S10]

**Implication:** an important African comparator for age-focused storytelling and multilingual delivery, with different languages and context. Do not repeat its categorical vulnerability claims about older people as established research.

### The Cyber Helpline: a real South African pilot, with bounded capabilities

An official announcement dated **5 October 2026** describes a 12-month South African pilot with MiDO Academy, The Cyber Helpline UK and AWS. Its service combines automated intake/self-help, trained human responders and referrals for affected individuals and sole traders aged 13+. It explicitly does not recover money or investigate crimes. “Intelligent chatbot” does not establish a particular AI architecture; a supported South African-language list was not verified. [S11]

The general service page includes South Africa, UK and USA for human support and aims for responses within three working days. That is not an immediate rescue service during a banking transaction. No South African pilot evaluation was located, and global historical reach should not be attributed to this pilot. Public guides are available; source/playbook reuse permissions were not established. [S12]

**Implication:** complex incidents can require people and referral pathways. An educational proof of concept should not promise the capabilities of an incident-response service.

### SABRIC and bank-specific guidance

SABRIC's public safety and resources pages cover ATM crime, social engineering, smishing and phone-related threats, with awareness materials. English pages were inspected; neither a comprehensive language catalogue nor AI-based education was established. No intervention-specific controlled outcome study was found. Some guidance warrants technical review before reuse, including categorical password-manager avoidance and routine password-change advice. Institutional provenance does not make every instruction universally appropriate. No open content licence was established. [S13]

Standard Bank's public Banking App Help Centre provides instructions for registration, beneficiaries, transfers, PayShap and security features. These are actual-bank guides, **not a safe-money sandbox**. English was verified; other languages, readership and learning outcomes were not established from the index. No AI is needed to deliver these guides, and an open adaptation licence was not located. Interfaces and bank-specific rules can change. [S14]

**Implication:** clear official guidance is an essential comparator. A practice intervention should show a benefit beyond finding or following existing instructions, without impersonating a bank or handling real credentials.

### Yima and participatory storytelling: adjacent rather than interchangeable

SAFPS's Yima advertises scam explanations, website-security checks, reporting/assistance links and a browser extension. Its scanner was not executed; detection accuracy, adoption, AI architecture and languages beyond inspected English were not verified. No reusable code licence was located. A technical website score should not be treated as proof that a business is legitimate—this is a methodological caution, not a measured Yima failure. [S15]

African community storytelling research provides situated design and ethics methods, not ready-made safety software or permission to reuse participants' accounts. See B6–B7 in [problem evidence](problem-evidence.md). [S16], [S17]

## Consequences for refinement

No inspected comparator establishes that the proposed AI functionality is necessary. Start with the user problem and compare against plain guidance, non-AI authoring and facilitated human support. BankBox provides a method worth studying, not a shortcut to efficacy claims or a licence to copy. Existing local narrative campaigns weaken a generic “we need scam stories” rationale; they do not settle whether community authors have sufficient control over language, revisions and reuse.

For Direction B, the evidence justifies investigating author-side needs as well as audience learning. It does **not** establish unmet demand or uniqueness. For Direction A, measure the particular step that fails and whether learning transfers beyond an imitation interface. Detailed proposed refinements and rejection criteria are in the [direction assessment](capstone-direction-assessment.md).

Additional leads, not relied on: Nedbank MoneyEDGE and older FSCA financial-literacy reports appeared in primary search results, but retrieval was incomplete. They should not be counted as fully assessed programmes. No further product inventory is necessary before discussing the main decision.
