# Community cyber reporting, collective intelligence and institutional response

> Historical research extension: the participant subsequently approved the connected journey now implemented in the [application](../README.md). Proposed studies and AI capabilities below were not thereby implemented or validated.

Research date: **9 November 2026**. Status: **focused extension of the owner's clarified Direction B; no final capstone selection or prototype implementation**.

This extends, rather than replaces, [problem evidence](problem-evidence.md), [existing solutions](existing-solutions.md) and the [direction assessment](capstone-direction-assessment.md). The owner clarified that the intended value is making lived digital-safety experiences useful to other people and responsive institutions. Authorship, consent and privacy are safeguards, not an assumed demand for a storytelling editor.

## 1. Executive findings

**The clarified direction is a credible subject for a bounded proof of concept.** Reporting barriers are documented, and operational systems show that consumer accounts can support institutional intelligence. That does not establish unmet South African demand, superior early detection or guaranteed response. Lack of a directly equivalent controlled study is neither a reason to dismiss the idea nor evidence of novelty.

The owner's HelloPeter and leaking-water-pipe analogies identify a practical purpose: describe an experience, find related accounts, direct information to someone able to act, and see what happens. The earlier recommendation overemphasised editorial ownership. It should now be qualified around **accessible experience reporting, discovery of related incidents, careful community warnings and visible institutional follow-up**.

| Evidence question | What this extension supports | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Does the problem exist? | Reporting-route uncertainty, adverse reporting experiences and underreporting appear in research, including a multi-country survey containing South Africa. [R1], [R2], [R3], [R4] | Local prevalence and which barriers matter most for the initial participants. |
| B. Do services adequately address it? | Public reviews/replies, private fraud reporting, aggregation and AI summaries already exist. [P1], [P3], [P5] | Whether a specific group needs a better combination, language experience or follow-up process. Feature overlap is not proof of adequacy. |
| C. Is the mechanism plausible? | Reports have contributed to operational disruption; complaint disclosure and clearer narratives have some empirical support. Verification delays and AI errors supply counterevidence. [I1], [I4], [I5], [A1], [A2] | Whether this implementation yields earlier, more accurate or more actionable information than simpler alternatives. |
| D. What requires testing? | A small workflow demonstration is feasible without live institutional integration or collecting sensitive incidents. | Willingness to report, public/private preferences, meaning preservation, useful corroboration and institutional uptake. |

**Important distinctions:** a report is not a verified event; similar reports are not necessarily independent; an impersonated organisation is not necessarily responsible; notification is not investigation; a reply is not resolution. Useful collective intelligence can be privately shared while selected, redacted experiences inform the public. Neither mandatory publicity nor permanent secrecy should be assumed to match contributors' needs.

### Scope and method

One targeted public-source extension in Default Mode, with two research agents covering platform/institutional models and collective intelligence. The parent review covered barriers, AI/language evidence, synthesis and the original brief. No accounts, survey responses or private incidents were accessed; no dataset was downloaded or reporting service operated. Sources were selected for mechanisms and contrasts, not an exhaustive product inventory. “Not established” means not verified in this inspection, not proven absent. Source dates, versions, methods and retrieval limits appear in section 10.

## 2. Reporting barriers and motivations

### South African evidence and transfer limits

Houtti et al.'s 2024 survey included **8,369 respondents in 12 countries, including 773 in South Africa**. Although its abstract uses national-representativeness language, its methods target the **adult internet population** in South Africa, using an online panel; it cannot represent people without internet access. Findings include uncertainty about reporting mechanisms in South Africa and other less affluent countries. The reported 37% non-reporting figure is an average across countries, not a South African population estimate. Self-reports about recent scam experiences do not demonstrate that a new platform would increase reporting. [R1]

The more detailed evidence about emotional and procedural barriers comes largely from other countries. It informs questions, not assumed local answers:

| Barrier or motivation | Evidence and interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Shame, embarrassment and fear of judgement | Documented in UK interviews and [Australian reporting experiences](https://www.aic.gov.au/crg/reports/crg-2913-14); reducing blame is worth testing, but public disclosure could increase exposure rather than reduce shame. [R2], [R3] |
| Unclear reporting route and complexity | Route uncertainty has South African evidence. UK interviewees described fragmented paths and uncertainty about who could help. [R1], [R2] |
| Low expectations of response | UK interviewees doubted police capacity or financial recovery. Providing honest status and limits may matter more than adding another intake form. [R2] |
| Retaliation or further harm | Some older UK interviewees feared repercussions or additional distress. This is not a measured South African rate. [R3] |
| Privacy and public visibility | The consequences of disclosing a narrative differ from confidential reporting. Whether privacy concerns deter the intended contributors remains a local validation question. |
| Language, literacy and device access | Plausible barriers for the proposed audience; the inspected studies do not quantify their contribution to South African non-reporting. Do not equate translation availability with usable reporting. |
| Usefulness and solidarity | UK participants described protecting others, civic duty, justice and recovering money as motives. Do not imply a community platform can return funds. [R2] |

Counterevidence matters: Koning, Junger and Veldkamp's population-panel analysis of 608 fraud events found severity and urbanisation consistently associated with police reporting; the inspected abstract does not establish shame as a universal independent predictor. Its institutional context differs from South Africa. [R4]

**Proposed encouragement, not proven interventions:** plain-language routing; a short initial account with optional later detail; supportive wording; assisted reporting with permission; clear choices about public visibility; acknowledgement and explainable progress; and a way to contribute an attempted scam without a financial loss. A witness, a victim and someone repeating a news item should have distinguishable roles. Public pseudonymity must not be called anonymity if the service retains contact details.

Private reporting may serve recovery or investigation; public sharing may serve warning, solidarity or accountability. Allowing one does not authorise the other. Test whether contributors want a private report, a public redacted account, or both, without asking them to disclose an actual incident during initial usability work.

**SABRIC is not interchangeable with a general public complaint desk.** Its inspected contact page labels its complaint mailbox for SABRIC's own conduct/services. Its banking-sector coordination role does not establish a public community-report integration. [SABRIC contact guidance](https://www.sabric.co.za/contact-us/).

**Policy counterexample:** on **14 August 2026**, CFPB announced the end of routine publication of unverified narratives and visualisations in the Consumer Complaint Database, citing misleading inferences and reputational harm. Its newer data-use page retains selected metadata and private supporting documents. The announcement also discusses narratives through its FOIA Reading Room/disclosures: this is **not a promise that every narrative is confidential**. Older opt-in narrative guidance is superseded for that database presentation. The agency's assertion of minimal utility is a policy position, not a controlled finding that public reporting never helps. [Dated policy announcement](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/the-cfpb-to-cease-discretionary-publication-of-complaint-narratives-and-visualizations/), [P5].

The design lesson is to separate public experience sharing, confidential evidence, intelligence coordination and formal reporting. A user should know which destination actually received which information. Institutional notification must not be advertised as a police report, regulatory filing or compliance certification.

## 4. Crowdsourced intelligence and early-warning evidence

### What has worked operationally

Australia's National Anti-Scam Centre Job Scam Fusion Cell combined consumer reports, industry intelligence and practitioner review during September 2024–March 2025. Its report links consumer wallet details to exchange action and records **836 wallets referred**, roughly **29,000 Meta accounts removed** through shared intelligence and **1,850 websites/adverts referred for removal**. Referrals are not completed takedowns. These are operator-reported outputs, not a controlled estimate of losses prevented. The report also describes encrypted services' reluctance to act on externally supplied screenshots because they could be doctored. [I1]

NASC's collaboration page distinguishes existing data feeds from a high-frequency sharing system still being developed. Do not present planned functions as deployed. The operational example supports the proposed information-to-action mechanism, but depends on trusted recipients and evidence they can use. Private aggregation can contribute without publishing every person's account. [I2]

### Why aggregation is not automatically early warning

GAO's 2025 audit documents inconsistent categories, incorrect consumer classification, duplicate grouping and variable underreporting/loss capture across US systems. Count comparisons therefore need definitions and context. [I3]

Moore and Clayton's PhishTank study offers direct counterevidence to automatic crowd superiority. Across 2007 submissions and a four-week feed comparison, crowdsourcing added complementary coverage, but verification of overlapping ordinary URLs lagged the proprietary feed by roughly **16 hours**, despite slightly earlier submission. Concentrated participation and correlated errors undermined the assumption that votes were independent. This is historical URL-based evidence, not a present-day service assessment or a trial of South African narratives. [I4]

Useful earlier recognition requires both **lead time** and **acceptable error rates**. More incoming reports can be useful even without proving a new campaign, but an apparent spike should trigger review rather than an accusation.

### Proposed checks for candidate patterns

These are design/measurement inferences from the evidence, not validated project features:

| Alternative explanation | What a prototype should distinguish |
| --- | --- |
| Duplicate submissions | Raw submissions versus distinct accounts/episodes; preserve a reason for merging and permit reversal. |
| Viral coverage or copying | First-hand experience versus news/forwarded content; incident date versus submission date; shared text is not independent corroboration. |
| Coordinated false reports | Timing/source concentration and repeated templates; review rather than treating vote volume as truth. Avoid automatically excluding people sharing a device. |
| Misclassification | User's account versus reviewer labels; preserve an unknown category and correction path. |
| Seasonal variation | Compare relevant periods only where enough history exists. A short demo cannot estimate seasonality reliably. |
| Changing participation | Show reporting-channel, outreach and form changes; do not call a growing user base a rising population scam rate. |
| Superficial similarity | Compare channel, pretext, requested action, time and independently provided indicators. A shared bank name can reflect impersonation, not bank fault. |

Useful corroboration adds independently observed, compatible detail; it is not merely clicking “same happened to me.” Screenshots may help an authorised reviewer but can be altered and can expose bystanders. Do not solicit real account numbers or identity documents for a public demo.

**Illustrative alert wording, not a real finding:** “Several independently submitted accounts describe a similar request during this period. Their connection and the responsible party remain unverified.” Use this only when independence was actually checked. Include the reviewed pattern, time window, limits, safe verification advice, last update and a correction route. Never infer a data breach, negligence or criminal guilt from a cluster. An uncertainty label does not by itself make identifying allegations safe to publish.

## 5. Institutional responsiveness

There is evidence beyond platform marketing. Mazur's 2022 dissertation analyses the first public CFPB complaint disclosure using a generalised difference-in-differences design, including 92 continuously supervised banks over 2011–2016. Greater complaint exposure was associated with increased mortgage approvals, especially under stronger regulatory scrutiny. This suggests disclosure plus oversight can affect behaviour, subject to observational identification assumptions. It is not evidence of scam recovery, South African institutional uptake or what a student platform can compel. [I5]

The earlier-described NASC interventions similarly required recipient organisations capable of investigating and acting. The CFPB publication-policy reversal supplies a counterpoint: an institution may value complaint intelligence while rejecting a particular public presentation. Neither observation settles the owner's proposed balance.

**Potential incentives to validate:** reputation, lower repeated-support costs, credible evidence of impersonation, complaint-management obligations and early visibility of operational failures. Potential barriers include privacy restrictions, unverifiable evidence, unsuitable submission formats, liability concerns, workload and the absence of an agreed receiving team. These are stakeholder questions, not confirmed reasons specific South African institutions will participate.

A proportionate response record should distinguish:

1. **Submitted:** the platform received the account.
2. **Delivered:** an identified recipient/channel accepted delivery; this alone is not acknowledgement.
3. **Acknowledged:** an authorised recipient confirmed receipt.
4. **Under review:** the institution states it is investigating; show who supplied this status.
5. **Action reported:** the institution describes action, with a public-safe explanation or private response.
6. **Outcome confirmed, disputed or unknown:** the contributor's response and any independent verification remain distinct from institutional closure.

Proposed safeguards include verified organisational representatives, scoped private evidence, recorded moderation reasons, corrections/appeals and a visible distinction between a reply and a resolved incident. Organisations should not receive unilateral power to erase criticism. Conversely, a right of reply is not permission to reveal a complainant's identity. “No response yet” means no recorded response, not guilt. A prototype can simulate this workflow with labelled demonstration roles; actual willingness, service levels and integrations require separate agreements.

## 6. AI opportunities and limitations

AI is a candidate aid, not the central justification. Hellopeter already documents AI summaries. The relevant question is whether an assistive step improves fidelity, accessibility or triage beyond forms, search, rules and human review.

**Evidence for assistance:** Shin, Kim and Shin analyse over a million US financial complaints and run presentation experiments. Experiments with 301 participants and 400 finance-experienced participants found clearer/LLM-edited complaints received higher **hypothetical compensation ratings**, not randomised real payouts. The observational analysis uses inferred AI authorship and assumptions that cannot eliminate all confounding. This supports testing faithful clarification, not embellishing allegations or promising redress. [A1]

**Evidence against autonomous labelling:** Chadalavada et al. developed a scam/fraud classifier on 300 labelled CFPB complaints already categorised as fraud/scam. An independent sample of 133 predicted scams yielded high precision, but results on broader complaints fell to .25 precision/.33 recall. Errors included treating claim denials, company reputation or poor service as scam evidence. Labels generally accepted narratives rather than independently verifying events. This is limited preprint evidence for narrow assistance, not a truth detector. [A2]

**Language evidence:** MasakhaNER 2.0 includes isiZulu within a 20-language entity-recognition benchmark, but news-domain person/organisation/location/date detection is not complete privacy redaction of informal incident accounts. A code-switching translation study found useful LLM performance across seven datasets, with variation by language/resource setting; it does not validate this project's isiZulu-English incident workflow. Language-specific, bilingual checking remains necessary. [A3], [A4]

| Possible assistive task | Simpler baseline | Meaningful comparison and failure checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Help describe an incident | Plain prompts and a short structured form | Completion effort and missing relevant details; preserve the speaker's uncertainty and meaning, rather than enforcing formal English. |
| isiZulu/English/mixed-language access | Original text plus human-assisted clarification | Bilingual review of names, negation, chronology and who did what; keep the original and let the contributor reject changes. |
| Structured extraction | Manual categories, dates and keyword rules | Field-level precision/recall and source-text support; unknowns remain unknown, never invented. |
| Sensitive-detail detection | Obvious-pattern rules plus manual review | Missed identifiers and unnecessary removal; contextual re-identification and screenshot metadata are separate risks. |
| Find similar accounts | Search/filtering, taxonomy and entity matching | Retrieval precision, missed links, false merges and source independence; similarity is a review candidate. |
| Candidate patterns | Human review with simple time/category counts | Temporal hold-out, false alerts and detection delay under changing participation; no proof from a compelling summary. |
| Community/institutional summary | Reviewed template drawing from selected records | Every claim traces to permitted input; distinguish assertion, evidence and response; measure correction effort and unsupported allegations. |

These are options for evaluation, not approved features or a selected model stack. Prefer constrained summaries, quotations/spans supporting extracted fields and human approval before release. Treat narratives and attachments as untrusted input, including instructions that attempt to bypass consent or leak other reports. Restrict retrieval to the reader's permissions. Do not send private text to an external model without explicit permission and an agreed data-handling approach.

AI can make a false accusation more fluent, merge unrelated incidents, mistranslate responsibility, expose identity or make coordinated reports appear independent. Human review can also fail. Evaluate the combined workflow and allow abstention; do not display an arbitrary model confidence score as the probability a person or organisation committed a crime.

## 7. Capstone alignment

The original *Capstone Brief* (participant-supplied PDF, not redistributed), pages 1–3, remains authoritative. This is an evaluation of the owner's clarified concept, not an AI-generated replacement. The software can support storytelling through understandable, discoverable and actionable lived experience; editorial ownership need not be the dominant value.

| Track | Credible alignment | Condition to demonstrate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voices & Languages | Ordinary people explain events in their own language, including mixed language. | Meaning and voice survive clarification; language support is actually reviewed. |
| Discovery & Audience | People find relevant experiences; appropriate community and institutional audiences receive usable accounts. | Discovery preserves uncertainty, permissions and context instead of broadcasting accusations indiscriminately. |
| Creation & Craft | Assist a contributor in expressing an intelligible account and relevant sequence. | Assistance supports the human account; a generic mandatory-field form alone does not demonstrate this. |
| Truth, Authorship & Ownership | Distinguish accounts, corroboration, corrections and institutional responses; retain consent and provenance. | Do not turn authenticity of the contributor into verification of every event, or a summary into adjudication. |

**Revised interpretation:** Discovery & Audience and Voices & Languages may now be as central as the earlier author-control tracks. Truth, Authorship & Ownership remains a safeguard and possible explicit track. The fit is stronger if a demo makes the journey from a person's experience to relevant understanding and response visible. An opaque risk dashboard or generic ticket form with stories stripped out would have weaker alignment.

The owner retains final concept/track selection. No claim of organiser approval is made. Earlier findings that stories are not universally better lessons remain valid, but educational superiority is not a prerequisite for investigating reporting and accountability value. The urgency hypothesis and BankBox limitations are unchanged and need not be researched again for this extension.

## 8. Evidence gaps and practical validation

A large formal study is not a prerequisite for a safe proof of concept. The following are **four proposed activities**, subject to approval; none was conducted here. Suggested numbers are manageable starting scopes, not statistically powered samples.

| Activity | Small starting scope | Decision it informs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting-choice walkthrough | About 4–6 potential contributors using fictional scenarios; include differing language/support needs. Do not solicit personal incidents. Compare private, public-redacted and combined routes. | Motivation, expected benefit, visibility comprehension, stigma concerns and routing clarity. Record disagreement, not just favourable feedback. |
| Recipient walkthrough | About 1–2 appropriately authorised fraud, consumer-support or community-support practitioners; contact only after approval. Use synthetic examples. | Minimum useful detail, evidence/channel requirements, authority to respond and an achievable acknowledgement process. Interest is not an integration agreement. |
| Small blinded comparison | Approximately 20–30 deliberately varied synthetic/owner-approved cases; bilingual review where needed. Hold some examples out of prompt/rule tuning. | Compare search/rules/manual work with optional AI on omissions, false merges, sensitive-detail leakage, unsupported claims and time to correct. Include near-matches, duplicates, copied news, contradictions and coordinated reports. |
| Feedback-loop and alert exercise | Labelled demo reports/organisations: one candidate pattern, one misleading cluster, a denied claim and a correction. | Can viewers distinguish reported, delivered, acknowledged, investigated and resolved? Can a moderator withhold/revise an alert and a contributor understand what was shared? |

For pattern recognition, potentially useful fields include incident/submission dates, contact channel, approach/pretext, requested action, outcome and separately permitted evidence. Public versions may need only a subset. Contact details, account numbers, identity documents, private messages, bystander identities and precise locations should not be exposed merely to make reports look credible. Establish necessity and retention before any later real collection.

Agree practical acceptance criteria before testing. Examples: no unsupported identifying allegation may be released; private material must not appear in public output; a person can correct the attributed account; the comparison reports both errors and successes. Withhold automation if its benefit over baseline is unclear. These are proposed gates, not test results.

A limited demo can establish workflow feasibility and measured performance on its test cases. It cannot establish national demand, reduced shame, representative incidence, genuinely earlier live detection, institutional cooperation, losses prevented or production security. Validation of those claims belongs to later, proportionate work—not fabricated outcomes or an indefinitely postponed prototype.

## 9. Implications for refining the owner's original concept

**Carry the clarified concept forward for the owner's consideration.** Frame its intended value as helping ordinary South Africans make digital-safety experiences understandable to others, connect relevant accounts and bring actionable concerns to appropriate organisations with visible follow-up. Preserve the owner's problem-reporting purpose rather than substituting a storytelling editor, tutor or simulator.

Potential differentiation lies in a **tested combination** of locally usable reporting, multilingual understanding, contributor-chosen visibility, corroboration that preserves uncertainty and a credible recipient feedback process. Existing products already supply many individual components. Whether the combination is valuable or underserved is a question for the small validation activities, not a market-gap assertion.

A possible narrow demonstration, if selected and approved, could follow a consented fictional experience into related-account discovery, moderator review and a labelled institutional response. Its purpose would be to make the mechanism inspectable, including a rejected false cluster. It need not publicly collect real allegations, integrate with banks or claim a live early-warning capability. This is refinement of the owner's direction, not implementation authorisation.

The immediate human decision is **which part of that reporting-to-response journey should the initial proof of concept demonstrate, for whom, and with what visibility choices?** The owner should first interrogate these findings and limits. Choosing a focus does not require proving the whole social mechanism in advance. The deadline constrains scope; it does not justify unsupported claims, missing consent or unsafe publication.

### Remaining inspection limits

No controlled South African evaluation of the complete reporting–correlation–response mechanism was located in this focused search. That is a coverage limit, not proof of absence or an argument against a prototype. No current law, platform terms or privacy configuration has been certified for a future deployment. Public allegations, evidence retention and external processing need review against the actual approved design before launch.

No source inspection establishes willingness of this project's potential contributors or institutions. The report offers a way to test those uncertainties without substituting unsupported promises for the owner's original purpose.
